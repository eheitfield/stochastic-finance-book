Preface

This book began as a personal project. Although I have a Ph.D. in economics and have spent nearly thirty years conducting research and applied work in finance, I never received formal training in stochastic calculus or continuous-time finance. As I engaged with this material in my work, I was often frustrated by the formalism with which it is commonly presented. Such formalism is necessary, of course, as a means of providing robust and complete documentation of complex and subtle ideas, but it can often be off-putting to those, like myself, encountering these ideas for the first time. I have long believed that the best way to learn a subject is to explain it to someone else. And so I decided to write this book to improve my own understanding of the subject matter and perhaps help others in a similar situation to myself.

Writing this book forced me to clarify concepts that I thought I understood, uncover gaps in my own knowledge, and connect mathematical results to the financial intuition that motivates them. My hope is that readers will find this perspective helpful. Rather than presenting the material from the viewpoint of someone who has taught stochastic calculus for decades, I have tried to write from the perspective of someone who knows what it is like to learn these topics for the first time.

This book is intended for readers with an undergraduate background in calculus, probability, and statistics. Some familiarity with differential equations and finance is helpful, but it is not essential. The book is written for practitioners, students, and researchers in economics, finance, and related fields who want to understand continuous-time models without first working through a graduate course in measure-theoretic probability.

My goal is to present the mathematical tools with enough detail to make them intuitive and useful in practice without overwhelming the reader with notation or unnecessary abstraction. Achieving that balance inevitably requires compromises. In several places I sacrifice mathematical rigor in favor of intuition, omit technical conditions, or present important results without proving them in their greatest generality. Whenever possible, I have tried to make these compromises explicit so that readers understand where we have cut corners.

The book is organized into three broad parts. The first three chapters develop the mathematical foundation needed for continuous-time finance. We begin with a review of probability theory and stochastic processes followed by an introduction to Wiener processes and Itô calculus. For readers primarily interested in financial applications, the first chapter may initially seem more abstract than expected. This is intentional. The concepts introduced there —- particularly the language of stochastic processes, filtrations, conditional expectations, and related ideas -— form the vocabulary of modern continuous-time finance. Becoming comfortable with these ideas, at least at a high level, makes the chapters that follow much easier to understand.

Chapters 4 and 5 serve as a bridge between the mathematics and its application to finance. Here we introduce the fundamental ideas of asset pricing through hedging portfolios and risk-neutral valuation, culminating in the derivation of the Black–Scholes model. Although the Black–Scholes framework is elegant in its own right, it also provides the conceptual foundation for many of the pricing methods used throughout the remainder of the book. In chapter 5 we dive deeper into the economics of valuing future cash flows when interest rates are uncertain.

The remaining chapters develop a series of canonical continuous-time models that build naturally on these foundations. We study interest-rate models for bond pricing, models incorporating jumps in asset prices, and stochastic volatility models, among others. Throughout these chapters the emphasis is not only on deriving the models but also on understanding what they imply. Whenever possible, new mathematical concepts are introduced through concrete examples before being generalized to more abstract settings.

This book is not intended to be the final word on stochastic calculus or financial engineering. Rather, it is meant to be a guide for readers who want to become comfortable with the core ideas that underpin modern continuous-time finance. If, by the end of the book, you feel confident reading the literature, implementing these models in software, and exploring more advanced treatments on your own, then it will have achieved its purpose.
